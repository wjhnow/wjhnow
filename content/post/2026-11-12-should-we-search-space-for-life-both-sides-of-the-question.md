*The Window We Look Through*

## The house with the holes

Imagine you find small holes in the basement wall and ceiling, and you think cats or rats could be coming in. Would you search the backyard first, or check inside the house?

That picture sits behind a question many people ask: why spend so much searching space for life when reality may hold more than we can see, right here around us? It deserves a better answer than a shrug.

It turns out there are two good answers that pull in different directions, and this article gives both.

### Our window is bigger than our eyes*

Our window isn't only what our eyes can see. It is our senses, our tools, our brains, and even our sense of scale.

The light we see is only a thin slice of a much wider family of waves. Radio waves, infrared and X-rays are part of that family too. They pass through the room you are sitting in right now, and we only learned they exist by building tools to find them.

Even "big" and "small" depend on where we stand. An elephant is huge next to an ant, small next to a mountain, and the mountain is a speck next to the planet.

Position decides what we see too. A car can sit in a driver's blind spot, fully real and right beside them, and stay out of view until a mirror or a turn of the head changes the view. Hold a sheet of paper level with your eye and it looks like a single line. Saturn's rings are so thin compared to how wide they are that they almost vanish from time to time, when we happen to see them exactly from the side. And we have never seen our own galaxy, the Milky Way, from outside, because we live inside it. Its shape was worked out by mapping stars and gas from within.

In each case the fix is the same: change where we stand, or add a second view. The thing being looked at doesn't change at all.

Evolution tuned our minds for medium-sized things moving at medium speeds. That is why the physics of the very small and the very fast feels so strange. So every guess about what exists, and what life can be, is made from inside this window. That cuts both ways, as we'll see.

### What science has shown, and what it hasn't

Things we can't see have turned out to be real, again and again. Germs were invisible until microscopes revealed them. Radio waves were predicted on paper first and found in the 1880s. Neutrinos, tiny particles that stream through your body in huge numbers every second, were proposed in 1930 and finally found in 1956. And in 2015 we detected ripples in space itself, a century after Einstein said they should exist. Each one needed a better window.

But the reverse has happened too. Scientists once believed in an invisible substance called ether that carried light. And in 1903 a respected French physicist reported a new kind of ray. Neither exists. So "unseen" is neither proof that something is there nor proof that nothing is.

Two popular claims need care. The famous 5% figure doesn't mean we only see 5% of everything.

It means that ordinary matter — everything we are made of, plus all the stars and planets — makes up only about 5% of what the universe contains, counting matter and energy together. A little over a quarter is dark matter and about two-thirds is dark energy. Those shares are approximate. Dark matter is invisible stuff we only know about through its gravity, since galaxies spin faster than the visible matter could explain. It appears to sit in ordinary space, not somewhere hidden. Dark energy is the name for whatever is making the universe expand faster. Nothing suggests anything lives in either.

Hidden dimensions are another popular idea. Some theories, such as string theory, say space may have extra directions we can't sense. Physicists take this seriously, but no experiment has found one. That makes it unproven, which is not the same as false.

### What is life?

Science has no single agreed definition. A common working one, used by NASA, says life is a chemical system that keeps itself going and can evolve over generations. Notice that this describes what a thing *does*, not what it is made of.

Water, oxygen and flesh are what life on Earth is made of and runs on, but they are not part of the definition. Think of a story: it can be written in ink, shown on a screen or spoken aloud, and it is still the same story. In the same way, if something uses energy, keeps itself going, copies itself and changes over generations, the definition doesn't care whether it is built from carbon and water or from something we have never met. That is why forms unlike ours are not ruled out, and why scientists hunting for life try not to assume it must look like us.

Some people go further and say that nature itself might be a kind of life, acting with purposes beyond our sight. Not only the living things inside it, but the whole of it, the way a body is more than its separate cells. It is an old idea, held for different reasons by different people, and it asks whether what we call "the environment" might be more than scenery.

A scientific cousin of that idea, the Gaia hypothesis, pictures Earth's living things and the planet as one system that keeps itself in balance, though most scientists treat that as a way of speaking, not a literal fact. It may equally be that nature has no purposes at all. We have no test for either, which also means no way to dismiss either.

### The case for leaving other worlds alone

The argument starts from humility. We look through a limited window, so we cannot know what our actions will cost, and some harm can't be undone. Invasive species on Earth show how a harmless-looking move can ruin an ecosystem. Moons like Europa and Enceladus may hide oceans under their ice, and carrying Earth's germs there could destroy something we'd never get back.

People lived for most of history without knowing about germs or atoms, so we don't need this knowledge to survive. And nature is older than us. If it acts with purpose, it knows what to move, when and how much, and we do not.

There is also a plain risk argument. If we hold back and nature has no purpose, we lose only knowledge, which can be gained later. If we act and we were wrong, the loss may be permanent. When one mistake can be undone and the other can't, caution costs less.

The challenge to this view comes in three parts. Nature already moves rocks between worlds, and pieces of Mars have landed on Earth as meteorites. If nature's purposes are hidden, we can't tell whether it wants us to explore or to stay home, and our curiosity came from nature too. And knowledge has changed lives, since science holds that clean water and an understanding of germs helped people live longer.

Those who hold this view answer that nature's moves aren't ours to copy, and that "we don't know" is reason enough for care.

### The case for looking

The argument here is that looking costs nothing out there. Telescopes and probes that only observe take nothing and change nothing. They do change where we stand, which is the lesson of the blind spot and the sheet of paper: a new viewpoint shows what the old one hid. Space telescopes are how we measured the 5% figure and mapped the faint glow left over from the Big Bang. We are also part of nature, and the curiosity that drives the search came from the same process that made everything else, so searching may be nature looking at itself. Every widening of the window so far has shown things nobody thought to ask about. And samples brought back can be re-tested for decades with tools that don't exist yet.

Cost is the usual objection, and it is real but not lasting. NASA spent about $25 billion in 2024, less than half of one percent of US federal spending, and most of that pays people and companies on Earth.

The challenge to this view is serious. Contamination, meaning carrying Earth's germs to another world, can't be undone, and no benefit repays harm that can't be reversed.

"It might help us" treats other worlds only as resources, while some hold that places have value for their own sake. Those who favor looking and sampling answer that the risk can be managed, through rules that clean spacecraft before launch and seal samples brought home — which lower the risk, but can't remove it entirely — and that ignorance has costs of its own.

### Where the two views meet

Both sides agree on a lot: the search isn't needed for survival, our window is limited, and nobody can prove what nature's purpose is, or that it has none. The real disagreement is where to draw the line. Drawing it at what we can see fails, because the window keeps moving, and almost everything we now know was once unseen.

A better line is harm: dig anywhere, but not where the damage can't be undone. Telescopes and flybys pass that test easily. Landing on a world, taking samples and bringing them home is where it gets hard.

The strictest reading says take nothing and change nothing. A middle reading allows sampling under tight containment. Notice what the blind spot and the sheet of paper had in common: the fix was to move the viewer, never the thing being viewed. The strictest reading asks for the same, to change where we stand and leave the world alone. Those who favor sampling reply that some things, like what a rock is made of or how old it is, can't be read from a distance.

Which is right depends on how much weight we give to the unknown against what we could learn. That is a judgment about values, not a fact science can settle.

It also depends on where we stand, the same way size does. An elephant is huge beside an ant and small beside a mountain, and nothing about the elephant changed, only the place we measured it from. A scientist, a person of faith and a farmer may each weigh "what we could learn" against "what could be lost" differently, and each is judging from a different standpoint.

*The takeaway*

Don't conclude "nothing" because we can't see it, and don't conclude "something" until there is a way to test it.

The same care applies to big ideas like hidden life or a purposeful nature: they may be possible, and "possibly" is a safer word than "claim" for both sides. Whether the holes lead anywhere, we can't yet say. What we can do is keep checking, and decide honestly how much we're willing to break to find out.

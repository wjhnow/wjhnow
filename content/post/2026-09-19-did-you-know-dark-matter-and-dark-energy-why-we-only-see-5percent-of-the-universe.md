<p class="has-dropcap"> This was the  headline that stopped my thumb mid-scroll: "Everything you can see in the universe, every star and galaxy any telescope has ever photographed, adds up to about just 5% of what's actually out there." </p>

It's not 5% of what we've discovered. It's 5% of everything. I had to read that twice. 

The other 95% is made of stuff that doesn't emit light, doesn't reflect light, and barely interacts with normal matter at all. Cosmologists split it into three pieces: normal matter (5%, everything made of atoms — you, me, every planet and star and whatever is within it ), dark matter (27%, invisible mass that holds galaxies together through gravity alone), and dark energy (68%, doing the opposite job, pushing space apart faster every year). What excited me is, If you scale the whole universe down to the size of a person, the 5% that's normal matter wouldn't even register as a speck of dust on their skin. Or picture it as an ocean — normal matter is a few drops on the surface. We are not what matters most in the universe.

And the dark energy part is the one that actually gets me, so let me stay on it a second. For most of the 20th century, physicists assumed gravity would eventually slow the universe's expansion down — the whole thing had been flung outward by the Big Bang, and gravity was supposed to be the brakes, maybe even the reversal. That was the working assumption for decades. Then in 1998, two separate teams of astronomers, working independently and not exactly trying to overturn physics, were measuring distant exploding stars called Type Ia supernovae. These explosions burn at a known, fixed brightness, which makes them useful as cosmic distance markers — how dim one looks tells you how far away it is. Both teams expected to find the expansion slowing down, exactly as predicted. Instead they got the opposite. It's speeding up. Not slowing, not steady — accelerating, and doing it faster the longer you watch.

Sit with that for a second, because it's a genuinely strange result. Nobody built a model expecting acceleration. It just showed up in the data, from two teams who weren't looking for it, and it held up. That discovery won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2011 and we still don't have a real explanation for it. "Dark energy" is not a real description. It's just a name scientists use for "we don't know what this is yet."

Dark matter's backstory is older and, in a weird way, calmer — less "the universe just did something inexplicable" and more "the numbers didn't add up." An astronomer named Vera Rubin was measuring, in the 1970s, how fast stars orbit within spiral galaxies. Basic physics says stars farther from a galaxy's center should move slower than ones near the middle, the same way Neptune crawls compared to Mercury. Rubin found the opposite — stars way out on the edges moving just as fast as ones near the core. The only way to explain that is if there's a lot more mass out there than anyone can see, enough gravity to keep those outer stars from flying off into space. We still haven't detected that mass directly. We know it's there the way you know it's windy by watching trees bend, not by seeing the wind itself.

As for who's chasing this now: Webb wasn't built specifically for this, but its view of extremely old, distant galaxies is filling in the timeline of how structure formed under dark matter's influence. Euclid, launched by the European Space Agency in 2023, was built specifically for this — mapping dark matter's distribution and dark energy's effects across billions of light-years. Roman, NASA's next big telescope, is aimed at similar questions.

We will not see dark matter or dark energy directly. We might never get to see them. That's where science is right now.

# Super CEO? Come on

I keep seeing this phrase everywhere now — Super Intelligence. SI. As if someone in Washington decided to just rebrand AI overnight and we all have to play along.

Which, apparently, actually happened. On September 29th, Trump signed that order — "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence" — and told every federal agency to stop using the word Artificial Intelligence. No more AI. Now it's SI. http://ai.gov becoming http://si.gov. The official reasoning was that "artificial" sounds fake, like it's counterfeit, and this stuff is real and powerful, so we should call it super.

I read that and just thought — this is exactly how a bad company promotes people. Like, textbook bad management.

Think about it. You hire this kid. Brilliant kid. Memory like a sponge, works 24 hours straight, never complains, can chew through a 100-page spreadsheet before you finish your coffee. He never takes a sick day. Absolute beast of an employee, everyone loves him. Do you make him the CEO? Do you hand him the keys to the building and say "you're the super boss now, we're all reporting to you"?

No. You don't. You say thank you, great job, now do the next task. You keep him in his role, because he is still working inside YOUR office. Using YOUR computers. Following YOUR budget and YOUR guidelines you wrote three years ago. He's maximizing your system. He didn't invent a new company out of thin air.

That's what we're doing with this SI thing. We're taking our own tool and crowning it as our superior. And it's weird.

I work with these models almost every day for work, and let me tell you what they actually are when you strip away the marketing. They don't wake up with ideas. They don't sit and stare out the window and go "hmm, what if E=mc3?" They don't get bored and decide to invent a new field of physics for fun. They are trained on... well, us. Billions of pages of us. Our books, our art, our code, our homework, our dumb arguments on Reddit from 2011, our Wikipedia edits. When it writes you a beautiful essay, you're not seeing a super genius at work. You're seeing a really, really polished, incredibly fast mirror of us. It's predicting the next most likely word based on patterns that we created over centuries of arguing and writing and building.

So in that company analogy that I keep coming back to — we are still the founders. The humans. We set the goal, we provide the data, we built the hardware in some freezing data center in Arizona, and we maintain the ultimate authority to pull the plug when it starts saying nonsense. The machine is the employee. A stupidly fast, incredibly useful employee who never sleeps, but an employee nonetheless.

My calculator can do math a million times faster than me. I never once thought my calculator was smarter than me, or that I should work for it. It's a tool. It needs me to tell it what to calculate. Same logic.

Now, the pushback I always get here — and I get it, I really do — is the intelligence explosion thing. The Bostrom crowd, the whole Silicon Valley futurist circle. The theory goes like this: once AI gets as smart as a pretty good human computer scientist, it will be given the task of improving its own code. And because it doesn't need sleep or coffee breaks, it could theoretically redesign itself over and over, thousands of generations of improvement in a matter of days or weeks. Each version smarter than the last. Boom, real superintelligence that leaves us in the dust.

And they point to stuff we see now that is a little spooky. Like when a model solves a coding bug using a pathway that its own creators can't fully explain. The famous black box problem. The developers look at each other and go "we don't really know how it did that." So they say, see? It's already beyond us.

Honestly? Even if that happens, even if it rewrites itself a thousand times in a week, it doesn't solve the core paradox. Because even if it rewrites itself a thousand times, where did it start? In our sandbox. Using our math. Our logic. Our data structures. Our Python libraries. If it discovers a new law of physics tomorrow, great, amazing, but it discovered it using instruments and concepts that we discovered first and fed into its memory banks. It have access to telescopes we built, data we collected.

It's finding connections between dots at a scale I can't even comprehend, sure. I will give it that. But we drew the dots in the first place. We drew them and numbered them.

What it doesn't have — and this is the part people skip when they get excited about SI — is three things. First, intent. It doesn't _want_ anything. It's programmed to reduce an error number to zero. It doesn't wake up and think "today I want to cure cancer." It is told to. Second, consciousness. It doesn't feel the joy of solving something after struggling for three days. It doesn't feel the weight of making a wrong call. And third, creativity out of nothing. Human breakthroughs are messy. They come from irrational leaps, from heartbreak, from a mistake in the lab, from seeing something random on the train. A machine operates strictly within its mathematical parameters, no matter how wide we make them.

So until a piece of software wakes up on its own, decides it wants to start its own company, feels something like embarrassment or pride, and creates something that has absolutely zero connection to any human data — which, let's be real, has not happened and is nowhere close to happening — it is not the boss. It can't be. It can't outrank the hierarchy that created it.

You can change the title on a government website all you want. You can call it Super Intelligence, Ultra Intelligence, Whatever-Intelligence. You can make all the executive orders you want. That doesn't change who built who.

We built it. It's the best, most transformative employee we've ever had in human history. It's going to change everything about how we work.

But we are still the ones who need to stay in the corner office. And we should probably remember that before we hand over the keys.
